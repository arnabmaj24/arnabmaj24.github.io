## The goal

Battery-powered quadcopters have a rough ceiling on how fast they can go before weight, power, and drag start fighting each other. Three of us set out to chase the speed record for battery-powered quads, using it as a reason to actually engineer around that ceiling instead of just building another FPV drone.

## My role

I owned the physical build: CAD and 3D-printed manufacturing for the airframe, the electronics and flight-controller configuration, and the actual test flying. The aerodynamic side, shaping the high-speed body through ANSYS testing, was a teammate's work.

## How I approached it

Every airframe component went through the same loop: design in CAD, print it, fly it, see what broke or what was heavier than it needed to be, and cut it down. Weight came out of nearly every part across enough iterations that the current build barely resembles the first one.

## Hardest problem

Flying it is a different problem from building it. A frame optimized down to the gram behaves differently in the air than a conservative one, and finding that out usually meant a crash, followed by reprinting whatever broke and figuring out why.

## Result

The project is ongoing, currently topping out around 104 km/h, iterating toward a faster build.

## What I learned

Optimizing for speed is really optimizing away margin. Every gram you cut also cuts into how much abuse the airframe can take before something snaps. Being the one who both builds and flies it forced me to actually feel that trade-off instead of just calculating it.
