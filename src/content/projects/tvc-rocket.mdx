## The problem

Passive fin-stabilized model rockets are simple, but they don't correct for disturbances in flight. Six of us set out to build a model-scale rocket with active thrust vector control instead, aiming for a propulsive ascent and a controlled landing rather than just stable flight.

## My role

Mechanical design and manufacturing was mine: the rocket body and the mechanism that actually gimbals the motor. I went through multiple mechanism iterations in Onshape, working against the optimization constraints and requirements the team had set for gimbal range, actuator mounting, and mass budget. I also worked alongside the team on the GNC side, contributing to the control implementation and tuning rather than just handing off a mechanism and walking away.

## How the system worked

The rocket's guidance stack fuses IMU and magnetometer data through an Unscented Kalman Filter to estimate pose in flight, feeding a PID attitude controller that commands the gimbal to correct trajectory. My focus was making sure the mechanism could actually deliver the range and speed the controller needed, and tuning the controller against what the hardware could really do.

## Hardest problem

The mechanical constraint that mattered most was fitting a gimbal with enough range and actuator authority into the mass and space budget of a model rocket, without adding so much weight that it worked against the ascent it was supposed to be correcting. Designing a mechanism to satisfy someone else's control requirements, instead of the other way around, is a different discipline than mechanical design in isolation.

## What I learned

A mechanism is only as good as its integration with the system commanding it. Gimbal range and actuator bandwidth aren't just mechanical specs, they're control requirements, and getting them wrong means the controller can't do its job no matter how good the estimation is.
