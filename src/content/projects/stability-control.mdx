## The problem

Aston Dynamics engineers the trailer brake products that Deutsche Hydrapro sells, the TowPilot and the Delta, and both run the control loop and sway controller on this page. Trailer brake controllers are normally wired into the tow vehicle and set up for it. This one has to work on any tow vehicle with nothing but power and the lamp lines on the 7/12-pin trailer plug. There's no vehicle data bus and no wheel speeds: whether the driver is braking has to come from the lamp circuits, and how hard the trailer is braking has to come from an IMU on the trailer itself. Working with that little vehicle data is the hardest part of the product.

## The control loop

The loop runs at 100 Hz as a FreeRTOS task on an ESP32-C6. It wakes on each completed ADC frame (30 samples at 3 kHz, so one frame every 10 ms). A cycle takes about 3 ms typically and about 9 ms in the worst case, and a cycle that misses its frame is counted and logged rather than silently skipped.

I first wrote it in MISRA C on ESP-IDF, because the ESP32-C6 wasn't supported by PlatformIO's Arduino framework at the time, and later migrated it to Rust. Early versions ran the control and calibration logic as async tasks. I moved the loop to a deterministic synchronous task, with Bluetooth commands reaching it through a non-blocking latest-value signal, so radio traffic can't stall a control cycle. Every output is safety-gated, and the firmware is designed to reboot on failure.

## State estimation

I wrote the state estimation in embedded Rust: an Extended Kalman Filter with TRIAD calibration, so the IMU can be mounted in any orientation, and a position EKF fed by a GPS parser I wrote from scratch, which drives the products' fleet tracking and mileage features. I also brought up the BMI270 IMU with my own SPI driver.

## Reading the tow vehicle

The controller reads the lamp circuits through the ADC and has to tell braking apart from hazards and turn signals on vehicles it has never seen:

- High and low thresholds with a dead zone between them, so a noisy signal doesn't chatter.
- Hazards are recognized by blink period (within ±20% of the calibrated rate) and duty cycle (30–75%). A 5-second calibration learns each vehicle's blink rate.
- A lone turn signal is excluded when left and right disagree for more than 15 ms.
- Many trucks drive their lamps with PWM. Brake lights regulated at 1 kHz and at 200 Hz still read as braking.

The ADC driver underneath is a teammate's. The signal processing on top of it is mine.

## Sway control

Trailer sway is a lightly damped oscillation of the trailer about the hitch, and on a bad day it grows. The detector watches yaw rate and lateral acceleration, and oscillation, energy and frequency-band checks all have to agree before anything happens. Their verdict feeds an asymmetric hysteresis latch (+1 for each 10 ms control cycle that looks like sway, −3 for each one that doesn't, engage at 8, release at 3), so it needs at least 80 ms of sustained sway to engage and releases within 70 ms of it ending.

To score the detector, I wrote a Python labeler that goes through the logs offline and marks an event as sway if it has more than two distinct oscillation peaks or its fitted growth rate is positive. Against those labels, the detector caught every sway event at 98% precision over 100+ events.

**[Try the interactive sway demo →](/sway-demo)** It runs a simplified truck and trailer in your browser, with and without a sway controller that follows the same logic.

To measure the controller, I ran steering-impulse tests: constant speed, one sharp steering input, then the wheel held still, at 70–140 km/h. I found out afterwards that this is close to ISO 9815, the standard car-trailer stability test. I fit a decaying sinusoid to each yaw-rate response and computed the damping ratio from it. Over 20 runs we averaged ζ = 0.80; the top competitor's product averaged 0.55 in the same tests, so 45% more damping. At ζ = 0.80 the counter-swing is only about 1.5% of the first peak, so a simple peak-to-peak log decrement can't measure it; the fit over the whole decay can.

## The loop that fought itself

Early on, the brake loop always ran away to 100% braking. I traced it to self-excitation: the trailer's own braking shows up as deceleration in the IMU signal the controller reads, so the output was feeding its own measurement. I designed compensation for that path. With it, the loop held stable through a full stop from about 150 km/h on the road, and indefinitely in simulation.

## Testing

- 170+ unit tests on the control loop: requirements and equivalence classes, boundary values on both sides of every threshold, and fault injection (a stuck sensor, outage recovery, NaN at gimbal lock, timestamps going backwards, buffer overflow, PWM-regulated truck lighting).
- Most of the hardware-testable scenarios rerun on real hardware through the team's hardware-in-the-loop rig, which a teammate built.
- A car-trailer simulation I wrote runs the shipped sway controller software-in-the-loop ([see the simulation](/projects/vehicle-simulation)).
- Full-scale vehicle testing in Australia: I tuned and verified the controller across 8 configurations on 2 vehicles and wrote the results up for the team.
- A wireless 100 Hz telemetry system I co-built with a teammate (ESP-NOW receiver in Rust, Python FastAPI server, SQLite logs, live WebSocket dashboard), used for about 500 hours of bench and vehicle testing.
