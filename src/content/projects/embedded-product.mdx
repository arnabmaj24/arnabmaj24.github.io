## The problem

The product is a small battery-powered sensor that talks to a phone over Bluetooth. Apart from the external housing, where I only did some of the design in Onshape, the whole technical system was mine: what it needs to do, what it's built from, the board, and the firmware that runs on it.

## Hardware

I started from the electrical architecture (power, microcontroller, sensors, Bluetooth) and the power budget, before any schematic existed. The board is a chip-down ESP32-C3 design: the bare chip laid out on my own PCB rather than a pre-built module. I chose the parts, drew the schematic and laid out the PCB in KiCad with manufacturing and cost in mind, and brought up each revision on the bench with a multimeter and an oscilloscope.

## Firmware

The firmware started in C on ESP-IDF and is now in Rust. It has:

- A Bluetooth GATT service for live readings and calibration.
- Low-power IMU modes and a software shutdown, which together get the battery to 8–12 months at its expected daily use.
- Calibration that persists in flash, through a small key-value store I wrote that serializes settings into fixed-size buffers with serde and postcard.
- Over-the-air updates over Bluetooth that recover from lost packets. An 8 KB ring buffer sits between the Bluetooth write callback and the task that writes flash. The device acknowledges cumulatively every 50 ms, sends a negative acknowledgement when it sees a gap, re-acknowledges as a keepalive, and checks the whole image's CRC before it's ever used.

I also wrote the bring-up and commissioning playbook: hardware and toolchain setup, building and flashing, what a healthy first boot looks like, feature checks (serial log, Bluetooth, a never-calibrated unit, a bare IMU test) and how to push an over-the-air release.

## Result

The product is in a 30-unit customer beta. Production is planned at about 5,000 units a year once the enclosure tooling is done.
